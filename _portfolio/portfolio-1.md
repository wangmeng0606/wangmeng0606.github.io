Mineral scaling remains a major challenge in reverse osmosis because it reduces permeability, lowers solute rejection, and can irreversibly damage membranes. My work examines how membrane surface chemistry changes not only how much mineral deposits, but also the structure, orientation, and coverage of the crystals that form.

<br/><img src='/images/Orientation.png'>

Gypsum scaling tests on bare and bovine serum albumin (BSA)-conditioned membranes showed that BSA-conditioned surfaces required longer to reach the same permeability loss—even though they accumulated roughly twice the mineral mass. Detailed characterization linked the different permeability-decay rates to crystal growth orientation rather than deposit mass alone. The BSA layer stabilized bassanite and disrupted the oriented formation of needle-like gypsum crystals, reducing surface coverage and slowing permeability decline.

This result highlights an important design principle: controlling mineral structure and orientation at an interface can be as important as reducing total deposition.
